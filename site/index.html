About me

I study how software engineering practices change when we take sustainability, organisational context and the people doing the work seriously.

I am a PhD researcher in Computing Science at the University of Glasgow. My doctoral work asks how sustainability can move from an organisational ambition into the decisions, tools and routines that development teams use every day. Rather than treating sustainability as only an issue of power consumption, I study its environmental, technical, economic, social and individual dimensions, including developer wellbeing and long-term maintainability.

My research is largely empirical and industry-facing. Through practitioner studies in financial services, I examine what teams find useful, what makes proposed improvements impractical, and how existing Agile workflows and organisational constraints influence change. I also work on human-centred AI, including participatory fairness assessments, LLM support for Agile coaching and human agency in AI-assisted creative work.

Alongside research, I lecture and teach software engineering, develop AI-supported learning resources, mentor student research projects and work as a software developer. These experiences inform my interest in research that can be understood, scrutinised and applied by practitioners.

Research focus

Four connected areas shape my research programme, from organisational sustainability to the design and evaluation of human-centred AI.

Making sustainability part of everyday engineering

Primary doctoral research

My central research question concerns how sustainability considerations become part of ordinary development choices, rather than a separate initiative that teams struggle to sustain. I explore how software engineers, team leads and managers define sustainability and how those definitions differ when they face delivery schedules, legacy systems and competing priorities.

The work draws on interviews, focus groups and longitudinal industrial research. It examines five interconnected dimensions of sustainability: environmental impact, technical longevity, economic resilience, social effects and the sustainability of individual working conditions.

Tools, feasibility and organisational adoption

Sustainable software engineering

Software tools may estimate energy consumption, suggest green refactoring or help teams understand resource use, but an academically promising tool is not necessarily practical at work. My research examines what it takes to install and approve these tools, which data they require, whether developers can interpret their output, and whether the output helps teams decide what to do next.

I also investigate adoption beyond initial technical feasibility. A tool may be technically possible to deploy yet still fail to fit review practices, ownership arrangements or the available time of a development team. This line of work asks how tools and organisations need to adapt to each other.

Sustainability-oriented process improvement

Agile practice and longitudinal action research

I study attempts to introduce sustainability discussions and improvement actions into existing Agile processes, including retrospectives. Following these changes over time helps reveal the difference between reaching agreement in a workshop and maintaining a new practice when workloads, dependencies or team circumstances change.

The goal is not to prescribe a universal checklist. It is to understand what teams can realistically do, how they negotiate trade-offs, and what support they need to turn a promising action into a sustained practice.

Human-centred AI and AI for software engineering

Collaborative research

A second strand of my work asks how AI systems can assist people without removing their ability to challenge, interpret or shape the result. I contribute to work on participatory AI fairness, including the design of tools through which people affected by automated decisions can formulate fairness criteria relevant to their experiences.

I also investigate LLM-supported Agile coaching and AI-assisted creative tools. Across these projects, I am interested in the boundary between useful automation and the human judgement, explanation and control that must remain visible.

Published and accepted research

Practical Feasibility of Sustainable Software Engineering Tools and Techniques

2026
Satwik Ghanta, Peggy Gregory and Gül Çalıklı | ACM FSE 2026, Industry Papers, pp. 829-839Published | First author

This paper asks a practical question: even if tools for sustainable software engineering exist, could developers in a regulated organisation actually use them? We evaluated representative tools concerned with energy consumption, green refactoring and workload management, focusing on installation effort, input-data requirements and the form of their results.

The work included workshops with 16 practitioners and a follow-up survey of 27 software practitioners. Participants favoured solutions that fit existing IDEs or delivery pipelines, demand limited and locally scoped data access, and return clear, actionable information. The findings illustrate why compliance, approval processes and time constraints are central to tool adoption, not peripheral details.

Perspectives, Needs and Challenges for Sustainable Software Engineering Teams: A FinServ Case Study

2025 conference
Satwik Ghanta, Peggy Gregory and Gül Çalıklı | ESEM 2025 Technical Track, pp. 196-206Published | First author

This industrial case study explores what sustainability means to people at different levels of a financial-services organisation. Through interviews and a focus group with six management participants and 16 software engineers, the study examined priorities, perceived responsibilities and barriers to sustainability-oriented change.

The findings exposed a meaningful contrast. Management tended to emphasise technical and economic concerns such as business continuity and cloud infrastructure, whereas developers raised issues such as workload and stress. Knowledge gaps, legacy constraints and scepticism about organisational motivations also emerged. The paper argues for context-sensitive approaches shaped with the people expected to use them.

Evaluating the Usefulness of Large Language Models in Learning Agile Coaching

2026
Satwik Ghanta, Ehikioya Obode, Lin Luo, Chris Hall, Tim Storer, Peggy Gregory and Gül Çalıklı | CSEE&T 2026Accepted for publication | First author

Agile coaching calls for more than recalling definitions: coaches have to interpret situations involving collaboration, competing needs and team practices. This research examines whether answers generated by large language models are useful for helping students reason through realistic Agile-coaching scenarios.

The work evaluates LLM responses against criteria relevant to software engineering education. It is intended to help educators identify where LLMs can support discussion and reflection, while avoiding the assumption that fluent responses alone demonstrate good professional judgement.

Preprints and submitted manuscripts

From Feasibility to Adoption: Perceptions of Sustainable Software Engineering Tools and Practices

2026
Satwik Ghanta, Peggy Gregory and Gül Çalıklı | Submitted to ICSE 2027 SEIPSubmitted | First author

This study extends the question of tool feasibility into the longer-term problem of adoption. Four focus groups involving 17 industry practitioners explored the practical conditions under which sustainability-oriented tools and practices could inform routine decisions instead of remaining isolated trials.

The work examines fit with existing workflows, the ability to inspect and review outputs, and the question of who within an organisation is responsible for using and maintaining a tool. It aims to explain why overcoming installation barriers is only one part of creating sustainable engineering practice.

When Sustainability Meets Practice: Challenges of Sustainability-Oriented Software Process Improvement in Industry

2026
Satwik Ghanta, Peggy Gregory, Gül Çalıklı and A. Salimy | Submitted to ICSE 2027 SEIPSubmitted | First author

This manuscript reports on longitudinal industrial action research following attempts to incorporate sustainability into Agile retrospectives and improvement work. Rather than focusing only on what participants intended to change, the study traces what happens to agreed actions as teams return to normal delivery demands.

It examines how workload, team dependencies and changing circumstances affect implementation. The focus is the gap between recognising sustainability challenges and finding the time, responsibility and organisational support to act on them.

Use of Generative AI for Sustainable Software Requirements Elicitation: An Exploratory Comparison of Students and Industry Practitioners

2026
Satwik Ghanta and collaborators | Submitted to Automated Software EngineeringSubmitted | First and corresponding author

Requirements elicitation is an early opportunity to consider sustainability, but participants may struggle to identify its different dimensions during brainstorming. This study compares conventional requirements discussions with sessions supported by generative AI, asking how the tool influences the range and quality of ideas that emerge.

The exploratory comparison involves four focus groups: eight MSc students and eight industry practitioners in total. The reported analysis suggests that AI can broaden initial ideation, while discussion among people remains important for critical assessment, contextual understanding and refinement.

What Changes When the Story Changes? Governing Revision in Multimodal AI Video Authoring

2026
Lin Luo and Satwik Ghanta (equal contribution) | Submitted to ACM CHISubmitted | Co-first author

This human-computer interaction project investigates a problem faced by creators using generative AI: changing one part of a multimodal story can alter related scenes, characters and narrative elements in unwanted ways. The AnimWeave prototype examines how people can direct which elements an AI system should revise and which it should preserve.

The project included a formative workshop with six creators and a user study with 12 participants. I co-developed the prototype, shared study responsibilities and provided overall research mentorship across planning, design, execution, analysis and writing. This was research-project mentorship, not formal supervision of a PhD degree.

Empowering Affected Individuals to Shape AI Fairness Assessments: Processes, Criteria, and Tools

2026
Lin Luo, Satwik Ghanta, Yuri Nakao, Mathieu Chollet and Simone Stumpf | arXiv:2602.06984Preprint available | TOCHI manuscript submitted

Fairness evaluations of AI systems are often defined by technical experts using predetermined measurements. This research instead explores how people affected by automated decisions describe fairness in their own terms and translate those concerns into criteria that can be evaluated.

In a credit-rating scenario, 18 participants used an interactive prototype to articulate and operationalise their own fairness criteria. The study reports a range of concerns about both outcomes and procedures, showing why meaningful participation may require tools that support different ways of understanding fairness rather than enforcing a single fixed metric.

Educational software and course innovation

I build experimental teaching tools as part of my educational practice, while keeping the distinction between a working prototype and evidence of learning impact clear.

Course-grounded AI Virtual Teaching Assistant

Professional Software Development | Independently developedStudent pilot in progress

I designed and implemented a student-facing assistant that uses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to answer questions about course-specific materials. Instead of relying on a language model's general memory alone, the system retrieves relevant information from supplied teaching resources and uses it to support its responses.

The implementation combines course retrieval with an open-source language model of approximately 20 billion parameters. It is being piloted with students to explore whether it can make course information easier to locate and understand. Student feedback, learning outcomes and overall educational effectiveness have not yet been formally evaluated; I do not claim to have fine-tuned the model.

Automated AI-supported lecture production

Professional Software Development | Independently developedStudent pilot in progress

I developed a workflow for producing supplementary lecture recordings from slide decks and slide-specific scripts. The workflow combines synthetic narration based on my voice, an AI-generated presenter and automated video composition, reducing the repetitive work involved in producing some kinds of recorded teaching material.

It can also produce shorter and multilingual variants of recorded instruction. The motivation is to offer students more flexible ways to revisit content, not to replace live teaching. Questions about learning outcomes, accessibility and student engagement remain subjects for evaluation rather than claims of demonstrated benefit.

Sustainable Software Engineering card-game workshop

SICSA | Workshop designer, organiser and facilitator

I designed and facilitated a discussion game that encourages participants to reason about the competing sustainability effects of software engineering decisions. Players assume roles including organisational advocates, a devil's advocate and members of the public, and they must respond to arguments from different perspectives.

Rounds combine discussion, voting, changing scenarios and limited-use supporting events. The purpose is not to teach one predefined correct answer: the design encourages critical reflection about how evidence, incentives and circumstances can change a sustainability decision.
